# Client presentation

Inherits `evidence-citation`.

## Structure

Ten to fourteen slides. Narrative arc:

1. **Title** with the client name and date
2. **Situation** where things stand, in numbers
3. **What worked** two or three slides, the strongest findings first
4. **What did not** be direct; a deck that only says good things is not useful
5. **Complication** the divergence or the risk, the thing they did not know
6. **Recommendations** one slide per recommendation, three at most
7. **Next steps** specific, owned, dated
8. **Appendix** the supporting numbers

## The one rule that changes everything

**The slide title is the message, not the topic.**

Bad: "Channel Performance"
Good: "Email converts at twice the blended average, on a tenth of the spend"

A reader who sees only the titles should get the whole argument. If they cannot, the deck is a data dump with headings.

## Per slide

- One message. If a slide needs two, it is two slides
- Five bullets maximum, ideally three
- A bullet is one line. Not a paragraph with a dot in front of it
- Every number carries an evidence ID
- **Speaker notes on every slide**, written as what you would actually say, not a restatement of the bullets

## Charts

Use native PowerPoint charts, never images. They stay editable, they look sharper, and the client can fix a label without coming back to you.

| Claim | Chart |
|---|---|
| Comparing categories | Horizontal bar, sorted by value |
| Change over time | Line |
| Composition | Stacked bar, never a pie with more than four slices |
| Relationship between two measures | Scatter |
| A single number that matters | No chart. Large text |

Never a 3D chart. Never a pie chart of six or more categories.

## Do not

- Open with an agenda slide. Open with the finding
- Use a chart where one number would do
- Include a slide that says "Thank You"
